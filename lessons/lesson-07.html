Compounded Alphabets: A Concept English Doesn't Have

Everything so far still maps onto something familiar: one letter, one sound, left to right. This lesson introduces something genuinely different — letters that stack vertically, not side by side, to form a single compound sound.

In English, letters only ever sit side by side. Dzongkha lets one letter physically stack on top of, or underneath, another to form a single compound written unit. This isn't decoration — it's a core building block of the script. There are two families: letters stacked above a base letter (Gochen), and letters attached below a base letter (Dogchen).

མགོ་ཅན་གསུམ། — The Three Gochen (Superscripts)

Gochen (མགོ་ཅན) means "letters that have another letter on top of them." Three letters — ར (ra), ལ (la), and ས (sa) — can each sit on top of a set of base consonants.

ར་མགོ་བཅུ་གཉིས། — Ra-go Chunyi (12 letters with 'ra' on top)

རྐ་རྒ་རྔ་རྗ་རྙ་རྟ་རྡ་རྣ་རྦ་རྨ་རྩ་རྫ

ལ་མགོ་བཅུ་ཐམ། — La-go Chutham (10 letters with 'la' on top)

ལྐ་ལྒ་ལྔ་ལྕ་ལྗ་ལྟ་ལྡ་ལྤ་ལྦ་ལྷ

ས་མགོ་བཅུ་གཅིག། — Sa-go Chuchi (11 letters with 'sa' on top)

སྐ་སྒ་སྔ་སྙ་སྟ་སྡ་སྣ་སྤ་སྦ་སྨ་སྩ

How Gochen letters are read

Read top to bottom:

རྐ — "ra ka ta ka" (ར་ཀ་ལྟག་རྐ) — "'ra' on top of 'ka' is 'ka'"
ལྐ — "la ka ta ka" (ལ་ཀ་ལྟག་ཀ) — "'la' on top of 'ka'"
སྐ — "sa ka ta ka" (ས་ཀ་ལྟག་ཀ) — "'sa' on top of 'ka'"

For all base letters in this list — not just ཀ, ཏ, ཙ — the ར superscript works the same way: it's silent. You always read straight through to the base letter underneath, as if the ར weren't there at all. So རྒ is read simply as ga, རྔ as nga, རྗ as ja, and so on. In spoken Dzongkha the superscript does subtly shift the tone of the syllable, but for reading purposes, the rule is consistent: ignore the 'ra' on top and read the base letter.

འདོགས་ཅན་གསུམ། — The Three Dogchen (Subscripts)

Dogchen (འདོགས་ཅན) means "letters propped up from below." Three letters — ཡ (ya), ར (ra), and ལ (la) — can each attach underneath a set of base consonants.

ཡ་བཏགས་བདུན། — Ya-tag Duen (7 letters with 'ya' below)

ཀྱ་ཁྱ་གྱ་པྱ་ཕྱ་བྱ་མྱ

ར་བཏགས་བཅུ་བཞི། — Ra-tag Chuzhi (14 letters with 'ra' below)

ཀྲ་ཁྲ་གྲ་ཏྲ་ཐྲ་དྲ་ནྲ་པྲ་ཕྲ་བྲ་མྲ་ཤྲ་སྲ་ཧྲ

ལ་བཏགས་དྲུག། — La-tag Dru (6 letters with 'la' below)

ཀླ་གླ་བླ་ཟླ་རླ་སླ

How Dogchen letters are read

Same top-to-bottom logic, base letter first:

ཀྱ — "ka ya ta cha" (ཀ་ཡ་བཏགས་ཀྱ） — "'ya' beneath 'ka' is 'cha'"
ཀྲ — "ka ra ta tra" (ཀ་ར་བཏགས་ཀྲ) — "'ra' beneath 'ka' is 'tra'"
ཀླ — "ka la ta la" (ཀ་ལ་བཏགས་ཀླ) — "'la' beneath 'ka'"

Unlike Gochen, where the base sound often stays the same, Dogchen frequently shifts the sound entirely — 'ka' plus 'ya' becomes 'cha'. This is the opposite pattern from what you just learned with Gochen.

One letter, two roles

Some base letters appear in both lists — they can carry a superscript and a subscript at the same time. In སྤྱ (meaning monkey), the letter པ carries a superscript and a subscript in the same syllable.

Why this matters

This is one of the more demanding parts of learning to read Dzongkha, precisely because there's no English shortcut for it. But once stacking clicks, entire categories of real words that looked like unreadable clusters before will resolve into single, pronounceable syllables.
