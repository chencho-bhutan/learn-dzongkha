Why Learn Dzongkha?

If you're reading this, chances are you already have some connection to Dzongkha — maybe you grew up hearing it at home but never learned to read it. Maybe your parents speak it fluently and you understand more than you can say. Or maybe you've never spoken a word of it, but something about Bhutan and its culture has pulled you in, and you want to go deeper than what a guidebook can offer.

Whoever you are, welcome. This blog is for you.

A language many people love but few can read

Dzongkha is the national language of Bhutan — spoken in homes, sung in songs, printed on currency and road signs, spoken in monasteries alongside classical Choke. But here's the strange gap a lot of us live in: many Bhutanese youth today, especially those educated abroad or in English-medium schools, can speak some Dzongkha but never learned to read the Ucen script it's written in. Diaspora families often lose the language across a single generation — understanding fades to hearing, hearing fades to a few phrases, and eventually only a handful of words survive.

That gap is what this blog is trying to close, one small lesson at a time.

Why I'm doing this

I'm building this blog because I believe language is one of the clearest threads connecting us to home, to family, and to a way of seeing the world that doesn't translate cleanly into English. I'm not a professional linguist. I'm someone who cares about this language staying alive and accessible, and who wants to build the resource I wish had existed when I was trying to learn.

Who this blog is for

Bhutanese youth who speak some Dzongkha but never learned to read Ucen script; diaspora Bhutanese reconnecting with a language their family speaks but they never formally learned; and anyone else curious about Bhutan and its language, culture, and way of life.

You don't need any prior knowledge of Dzongkha or its script to start here. Each lesson builds on the last, and I'll always include the Dzongkha script, a consistent romanization, plain English explanation, and — wherever possible — audio, so pronunciation isn't left to guesswork.
